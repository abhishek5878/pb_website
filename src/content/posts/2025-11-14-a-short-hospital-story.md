“Pass me the blanket, you dufferrr,” said Mr. Singh, our unofficial in-house news reporter.  
His mother had been in the hospital for two weeks after a cardiac arrest.  
Stable, yes.  
Safe?  
No one really knew.

People imagine hospitals as sterile, efficient, white coats, stretchers, etc.  
But at night, they’re something else, an oddly assembled tribe of strangers bound by worry and love.

You see them asleep in impossible positions:  
on cafeteria chairs,  
on the floor,  
on those rigid benches that feel designed to break your spine.  
Anything that lets their eyes shut for even five minutes.

Daytime drains them, money, hope, energy,  
but nighttime does something different.  
It strips away the noise  
and leaves only a quiet, durable tenderness.

Not sympathy.  
Not pity.

Just the simple truth that some love is unconditional,  
and that when the worst happens,  
people show up.  
Even if all they can do is wait in the dark.

I found myself on one of those benches,  
one of those oddly assembled tribe,  
waiting and hoping for any positive updates about my grandfather’s health.

Night time gave that “person space” to just exist.  
I used to just observe  
Mr. Singh and his shenanigans with his son at night,  
another uncle curled up in a corner watching reels on loudspeaker (goated feed btw),  
an entire family existing over a mat with bags as their pillows,  
all sleeping to be ready for the war next day.

That whole floor sleeping created their own small kingdoms  
slowly and steady,  
alliances for morning chai with the guards.

Those small mats, joined benches turned bed,  
all remained same for the time I was there  
kind of a night on repeat,  
people trying to discuss politics or life  
to ease up their mind.

First night, I hoped.  
Last night, hope felt weak.

What remained was duty , raw, rare, unconditional  
and that Dettol smell.  
People showing up.  
Sleeping on floors.  
Because love, sometimes, is just refusal to leave.

Not fear,  
just the quiet signal of a final goodbye.

I don’t remember much from our hospital-floor empire.  
Most conversations blur now.  
But one sentence stuck,  
corner uncle’s, who spoke little:

“We all thought we’d outsmart life.  
Turns out life has better writers.  
Let it happen, enjoy the plot.”
